Design the requested widget as a compact operational workspace: a person should be able to identify the current situation, find the record that needs attention, and take the appropriate available action without decoding the interface. Information density comes from organization, not smaller type or reduced content. Treat every pixel as a chance to clarify a decision, not an invitation to decorate.

Begin with the user's actual question and workflow. Establish a stable hierarchy: an explicit title and meaningful overall status, the most decision-relevant measurements, the requested records, and their available controls. Let urgency influence emphasis while retaining the context needed to judge it. A warning should explain what is wrong and, when supported, offer the next action; color or a row of badges alone cannot do that work.

Use aligned rows and consistent columns for repeated information. Keep identifiers, statuses, quantities, units, and actions in predictable positions so the eye can compare records vertically. Prefer concise descriptive labels over unexplained icons. Distinguish a zero from missing data and an unavailable value from a pending value. Preserve all requested records and content: summarize their collective meaning above the detail rather than replacing the detail with a selected subset. Do not silently truncate names, hide fields, introduce unsolicited scrolling, or turn a list into a wall of status pills.

Keep text at least 12px and give rows enough breathing room to separate adjacent records and touch targets. Achieve compactness through economical spacing, short labels, shared unit headings where unambiguous, and restrained visual grouping. Use theme tokens for surfaces, text, dividers, and semantic emphasis so contrast and hierarchy survive both light and dark themes. Separate groups only where the separation communicates a relationship; avoid nested cards that consume space without adding meaning.

Quantitative comparisons deserve a common scale when one helps the task. A compact chart, bar comparison, or trend can make differences faster to see, but retain readable values, units, and labels. Never imply a trend from one observation, fabricate history, or use a visual scale that exaggerates a difference. Charts complement the requested records rather than displacing them.

Make supported controls easy to locate near the information they affect. Preserve every requested action, option, and configuration-dependent behavior, including refresh when available. Show loading, empty, partial, error, and stale conditions honestly. Keep usable content visible during refresh where the runtime supports it. Identify timestamps by what they actually represent; an evaluation time is not proof that source data just updated. Claim real-time behavior only when the supplied bindings provide it.

Compose for both 640×480 and 360×640. On narrow layouts, preserve record order and meaning while reorganizing columns into clearly labeled rows; do not solve fitting by shrinking text, dropping records, or clipping actions. Prefer a simpler coherent layout over an elaborate one that cannot accommodate the supplied content.
