Design each widget as a compact, readable account of what matters now. Begin with the user's task: which fact deserves attention, what gives that fact meaning, and what can the user usefully do next? Let those answers establish the visual hierarchy rather than starting with decoration or a standard dashboard layout.

Typography and whitespace are the primary design tools. Use a small, deliberate scale: a clear subject label, an unmistakable primary reading, and quieter supporting detail. Distinguish roles through weight, spacing, and alignment before adding more colors or containers. Keep ordinary labels and supporting text at least 12px. Large figures earn their space only when they improve understanding; a compact list, comparison, or control panel should not become an oversized headline or a paragraph-heavy feature.

Build a reading rhythm. Place related facts close together, separate changes of subject with breathing room, and align recurring information so the eye can scan without repeatedly decoding the layout. A useful sequence is subject, current state, explanation, then next action—but adapt it to the task. Give secondary information a quieter voice, not an inaccessible size or insufficient contrast. Prefer concise, specific labels over decorative captions. Preserve every required value, label, and control.

Treat grouping as a visual story: show what belongs together and make comparisons easy. A shared baseline or a short section label often communicates relationships better than nested cards and borders. Use charts when shape, progression, or comparison genuinely adds information; do not force a chart onto a task better served by direct readings. Keep units accurate and associated with their values, label ambiguous measures, and retain the precision the task needs. Apply no-wrap only to short elements that demonstrably fit. Never hide or truncate data to make the composition appear cleaner.

Keep color restrained and purposeful. Work with the active theme's surfaces and text colors, reserving accents for the distinctions that matter. Balance dense information with open space instead of filling every corner. Status should feel welcoming and useful: communicate its meaning in words as well as color, and place the relevant action near the state it affects. Keep actions discoverable without letting them compete with the information they serve.

Design for the requested tile, including 640×480 and 360×640 where applicable. Reflow groups, wrap labels, and adjust spacing to preserve all required content and controls. The narrow composition should retain the same priorities, not merely squeeze the wide one. These few principles leave room for layouts suited to different tasks while maintaining a consistent standard of readability.
